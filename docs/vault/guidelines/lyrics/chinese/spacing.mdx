title: Spacing

A single space separates two sung phrases inside a line. It does the work a comma does in English prose, and it is the only mark Chinese lyrics use for it. About half of all lines contain one.

A space appears for one other reason: to mark the boundary between Chinese characters and a run of Latin text. The two uses look identical and mean different things, so this page covers both.

## Where the space goes

Aim for two phrases in a line, divided by one space. A line holding a single phrase is common and correct. Three or more phrases is allowed, but it usually means a line break was missed.

Use a space where the singer breathes, rests, or plainly starts a new phrase, and the break is not large enough to justify a new line. For when the break is large enough, see [Line splits](./splits.mdx#line-splits).

<SyllableChips

  ttml={`<p begin="1:06.138" end="1:11.949" itunes:key="L5" ttm:agent="v1">最美的願望 一定最瘋狂</p>`}
/>

The singer stops after `願望` and starts again at `一定`, so the space goes there. No other boundary in the line gets one.

A space does not require a silence. It can also mark a phrase boundary the singer moves straight through, and the spacing then tells the reader where the phrases divide.

<SyllableChips
  source={{ region: "hk", songId: "539838976" }}
  ttml={`<p begin="19.239" end="25.720" itunes:key="L1" ttm:agent="v1">今天我 寒夜裡看雪飄過</p>`}
/>

Do not put a space at every grammatical junction. A line with a space between each two-character word is harder to read than one with none, and it tells the reader nothing about the performance.

Keep a word or a fixed expression whole when either position fits the delivery. See [Keep connected characters together](./splits.mdx#keep-connected-characters-together).

## How to write the space

- Use one space, never two.
- Do not start or end a line with a space.
- Do not space out characters that belong to one continuous phrase.

### Use a plain space, not a look-alike

A non-breaking space (U+00A0) and an ideographic space (U+3000) look like a space and are not one. A zero-width space (U+200B) is invisible in every editor and still ends up in the file. All three survive a copy and paste out of a word processor or a lyric website.

| Character | Name | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `U+0020` | Ordinary space | Use this one |
| `U+00A0` | No-break space | Replace with an ordinary space |
| `U+3000` | Ideographic space | Replace with an ordinary space |
| `U+200B` | Zero-width space | Remove |

Check for them before delivery. A line that looks right and sorts, searches, or splits wrongly usually contains one. Characters have the same problem, covered in [Check for look-alike characters](./text.mdx#check-for-look-alike-characters).

## Latin script inside a Chinese line

Put a space on both sides of every run of Latin text, and capitalize its first word. This applies wherever the scripts meet, including a single borrowed word in the middle of a Chinese clause.

Chinese needs no space between words, so a Latin run pressed against the characters has no boundary at all and is hard to pick out at lyric sizes. The space supplies the boundary the script does not.

<CodeComparison
  correct={"緊緊相依的心如何 Say goodbye"}
  incorrect={"緊緊相依的心如何say goodbye"}
  source={{ region: "tw", songId: "218877967" }}

Apple Music writes both of those without the spaces, which is why they appear here as the version to correct rather than the version to copy.

Drop the space only where the run already sits at the start or the end of the line. A run that begins a line takes no space before it, and a run that ends one takes none after it.

Capitalize the first word of each run and leave the rest to English rules. `Say goodbye` capitalizes only `Say`, an acronym such as `IG` keeps its own form, and a proper noun keeps its own capitalization wherever it falls. Apply English rules to any punctuation inside the run, and see the [English punctuation rules](../english/punctuation.mdx) for the details.

A space between the scripts is not a phrase boundary. A line can carry both, and the spaces around a Latin run do not tell the reader that the singer breathed there.

## Numbers

Write numbers the way the lyric writes them. Chinese numerals and digits are both correct, and the choice usually follows whether the number is read as a word or as a string of figures.

Digits do not take the spaces that [Latin text](#latin-script-inside-a-chinese-line) does. Write them tight against the characters around them, and keep the treatment consistent for repeated references to the same number.

<CodeComparison
  correctLabel="Prefer"
  incorrectLabel="Avoid"
  correct={"同我跳8拍 不如大膽數到9"}
  incorrect={"同我跳 8 拍 不如大膽數到 9"}
  source={{ region: "hk", songId: "1571428113" }}
/>

A digit is legible against a Chinese character in a way a lowercase Latin word is not, so it needs no boundary. A number written out in Latin letters, such as `One` or `Twenty-four`, is a Latin run and takes the spaces.
