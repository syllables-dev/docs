---
title: Line and syllable splits
description: Where to break a line, and why a character is never split or grouped
---

This page covers two decisions: where a line ends, and which characters may share a timed interval. For the space that divides phrases inside a line, see [Spacing](./spacing.mdx).

A line sync only needs a start and an end for each line. A word sync needs a start and an end for every timed unit inside it. In Chinese that unit is the character, because one character is one syllable, and a character is never divided further.

## Line splits

Line splits decide what the reader sees as one phrase. They apply to every lyric, whether or not it is word-synced.

### Follow the vocal phrase

Start a new line when a melodic or rhythmic phrase ends, a distinct response begins, or the delivery moves into a new thought. Listen to the characters in context rather than splitting the text wherever a clause ends on paper.

A lyric line can contain a complete sentence, part of a sentence, or a single character. Do not add characters to complete a sentence, and do not join two clearly separate sung phrases to make the grammar read better.

A pause inside a phrase is not a line break. The singer rests after `哼着` before naming the song, but the verb and its object are one sung thought and belong on one line, divided by a space:

A short line that cannot stand as a phrase on its own is usually a sign that the break belongs inside the line. Reach for a new line when the thought changes, not whenever the singer takes a breath.

### Keep connected characters together

When the performance allows more than one reasonable break, choose the one that keeps a word or a fixed expression whole. Avoid splitting a two-character word across two lines, or separating a measure word from its noun, without a musical reason.

This applies to a space inside a line as much as to a line break. When either position fits the delivery:

<CodeComparison
  correctLabel="Prefer"
  incorrectLabel="Avoid"
  correct={"我的訊息被隔離 一場雨淹沒聲音無人接聽"}
  incorrect={"我的訊息被隔離一場 雨淹沒聲音無人接聽"}
  source={{ region: "tw", songId: "1866517055" }}
/>

The second version strands `一場` at the end of a phrase, away from the `雨` it counts.

Musical phrasing can override this. A line may end on a particle or a preposition when that is where the sung phrase ends.

### Keep line lengths natural

Chinese lyric lines are short. Ten characters is typical, and a line much past fifteen usually contains a phrase boundary that belongs to a space or a line break.

- Keep a short hook or response short when it is performed as a separate phrase.
- Split a long passage at a clear musical boundary.
- Do not pad a line so that it matches the length of the line above it.
- Do not insert line breaks solely to match the width of an editor or phone screen. Automatic text wrapping belongs to the display.

Compare repeated passages. Keep their line breaks consistent when the words and delivery are the same, and preserve changes in phrasing when the performance changes.

### Separate the song's sections

Work out where one part of the song ends and the next begins. Verses, choruses, bridges, and other distinct parts are separate sections.

A lyrics file has no blank lines. Each part becomes its own section, and the lines inside it follow one after another. See [Song sections](../../../ttml/sections.mdx) for the markup and the labels.

The example marks the boundary with a gap:

<CodeComparison
  correctLabel="Prefer"
  incorrectLabel="Avoid"
  correct={`哼着 那首過氣芭樂情歌
就任回憶輾轉反側

該如何 掙脫逃逸這引力`}
  incorrect={`哼着 那首過氣芭樂情歌
就任回憶輾轉反側

  source={{ region: "tw", songId: "1866517055" }}

Do not start a new section after every sentence or every change of singer. Keep unsung section labels outside the lyric text; the file records the name of a part as an attribute on its section.

### Separate vocal parts

Identify the lead and backing phrases before deciding their line breaks. A backing phrase can overlap the lead or continue after it; it does not have to complete the lead singer's sentence.

For line-synced lyrics, place a short backing phrase in parentheses after the associated lead line. For word-synced lyrics, put it on its own subline. See [Background vocals](./text.mdx#background-vocals).

<SyllableChips
  source={{ region: "tw", songId: "1576303097" }}
  ttml={`<p begin="2:11.074" end="2:15.072" itunes:key="L53984">君常違兮 國隳蕪 (烏有兮 歸處)</p><p begin="2:15.082" end="2:18.564" itunes:key="L53985">蓄讎怨增欷 (零落兮 困洿瀆)</p>`}
/>

The backing phrase answers the lead rather than completing it, and it keeps its own internal spacing.

## Syllable splits

Syllable splits only apply to word-synced lyrics.

### One character, one interval

A Chinese character is a single syllable, and there is no smaller unit to divide. Never split a character into parts, and never let two characters share one interval.

That removes most of what syllable splitting means in an alphabetic language. There are no boundary rules to apply, no consonant to place on one side or the other, and no word that needs breaking apart. Most lines need nothing beyond one interval per character, and a line built that way is a finished word sync.

### Do not group characters

Two characters sharing an interval means the highlight runs through a syllable boundary the listener hears, and no delivery makes that correct.

Apple Music groups characters anyway. Here `飄下記` is one group and `一字` is another, while every character between them stands on its own:

### A word is not one unit

A Chinese word of two or more characters is not a timing unit. Split it like any other run of characters whenever both characters have their own onset, and that is the normal case.

The same word can turn up grouped in one place and split in another within a single line:

### Repeated characters

Give each repeat its own interval. A repeated character is sung again, not held, so grouping the repeats hides the rhythm that makes them a repetition.

### Latin words follow English rules

A Latin word inside a Chinese line is still a Latin word. Split it by the [English syllable rules](../english/splits.mdx#syllable-splits) and follow that page for every decision inside the run. It is the one place in a Chinese lyric where a single written word is divided into parts.

The characters around it follow the Chinese rule as usual, one interval each:

`Ba` and `by` are identical on both sides. Only the Chinese characters change, because the two scripts are split by different rules in the same line.

The spaces around a Latin run are [script spacing](./spacing.mdx#latin-script-inside-a-chinese-line), not phrase boundaries. Do not read them as places the singer breathed, and do not add or remove one to signal a split.

### Check the whole song

Split the same line the same way when it is sung the same way, and let it differ when the delivery differs. A chorus that repeats unchanged carries identical groupings each time.
